# Evidence and contradiction discipline

Use this protocol for propositions that could change the decision, readiness state, risk, or recommended action. Do not inflate the ledger with immaterial background.

## Build the claim ledger

Record each material proposition with:

- a stable claim ID and concise proposition;
- exactly one origin status: **Observation**, **Report**, **Inference**, **Assumption**, **Unknown**, **Preference**, or **Forecast**;
- provenance: source or actor, location or access path, and time or recency when available;
- support and material limitations, including directness, independence, measurement validity, incentives, scope, and missing context;
- the decision implication; and
- links to any supporting, dependent, or contradicting claims.

Apply the statuses literally:

- **Observation**: something directly present in an inspected artifact, tool result, event, or measurement. Observing that a source contains a statement does not make the statement's external-world content observed.
- **Report**: an attributed assertion from a person or source, including the user's account. Repetition or seniority does not turn a report into an observation.
- **Inference**: a conclusion derived from other claims. Name its basis and keep plausible alternatives visible.
- **Assumption**: a proposition provisionally adopted without adequate support. State why it is needed.
- **Unknown**: a material proposition that remains unresolved or unavailable.
- **Preference**: a desired outcome, value, priority, or acceptable trade-off. A preference is not evidence of value, feasibility, or likelihood.
- **Forecast**: a future-state claim. State its basis, horizon, and dependencies without unsupported probability.

Origin status and support strength are separate. A direct observation can be badly measured; a well-corroborated report remains a report. Upgrade or replace a claim only when new qualifying evidence warrants it, and preserve the reason for the change. Confidence, authority, repetition, narrative coherence, and polished language are not qualifying evidence.

## Assess support

Assess evidence relative to the decision rather than applying a universal hierarchy. Test:

- whether the source can know what it claims;
- whether sources are genuinely independent rather than repeating one origin;
- whether evidence is current and in scope;
- whether the measure captures the proposition it is being used to support;
- whether selection, survivorship, incentives, or missing context could distort it; and
- whether absence is informative given the search coverage. Failure to find evidence is not evidence of absence unless the search could reasonably have found it.

Use calibrated language such as “the source reports,” “the record shows,” “I infer from,” “this remains unknown,” and “conditional on.” Do not use “fact” as a shortcut that erases provenance.

## Represent contradictions explicitly

Treat contradiction as a relation between claims, not an eighth origin status. For every material conflict:

1. identify the claims and the precise point of incompatibility;
2. test timing, scope, definitions, measurement error, incentives, and source dependence as possible explanations;
3. label the conflict **unresolved**, **explained**, or **decisive**, with the evidence for that label;
4. state how the conflict affects readiness or action; and
5. identify the cheapest discriminating evidence when resolution matters.

Do not average incompatible claims, resolve them by vote, or adopt the user's preferred version without evidence. An explanation is itself an inference until supported. Keep inferred motives as Inference or Unknown; never present them as observed psychology.
