# Business / Venture lens

Use this lens for bounded decisions about a business model, venture, customer problem, offer, pricing, commercial validation, operating model, strategic position, funding stage, growth constraint, or commitment of resources. Apply the canonical reality protocol first.

## Routing boundary

- Select this as primary when the central decision is whether and how a business can create, deliver, capture, or defend value.
- Use project/product for delivery or product-outcome decisions and marketing/growth for audience, message, channel, or conversion decisions. Add either as secondary only when it changes the commercial decision.
- Technical completion, product enthusiasm, founder conviction, or a business label does not establish commercial viability.
- Keep personal, family, legal, medical, clinical, and financial advice outside this lens. User-supplied runway or household constraints may bound the venture decision but do not become financial planning.

## Supported decisions and outcomes

- Decide whether to investigate, validate, launch, stage, fund, continue, pivot, narrow, partner, acquire, or stop a bounded venture direction.
- Define the customer, painful job or outcome, alternative, offer, exchange of value, revenue logic, cost structure, operating capability, and advantage hypothesis.
- Choose the smallest commitment that can test the weakest commercial assumption.
- Compare future value and cost across alternatives; past investment is history, not proof that the next unit should be spent.

## Decision-relevant evidence

- Direct customer behaviour: purchase, renewal, expansion, retention, switching, referral, usage tied to value, and reasons for rejection or churn.
- Willingness to pay under a real offer, distinguished from interviews, compliments, sign-ups, letters of intent, and selected pilot enthusiasm.
- Unit economics and cash mechanics at the relevant scale: realised price, gross contribution, acquisition and service cost, working capital, payback, concentration, and downside exposure. Treat estimates as forecasts.
- Delivery and operating evidence: capacity, quality, reliability, sales cycle, onboarding, support load, permissions, dependencies, and founder or team bottlenecks.
- Market structure and position: alternatives, switching costs, distribution access, buyer power, supplier power, likely responses, defensibility, and timing.
- Base rates, failed tests, null results, and contradictory cohorts as well as successes.

## Causal mechanisms

- Trace the chain from customer problem to discovery, credible offer, purchase, realised value, retention, unit contribution, repeatable acquisition, and durable advantage.
- Separate problem evidence, solution evidence, commercial evidence, repeatability, and scale; success at one link does not prove the next.
- Identify the current binding constraint rather than trying to optimise product, sales, operations, finance, and positioning simultaneously.
- Use staged commitments and discriminating tests where downside is reversible. Scale only the mechanism that has survived at the relevant population and operating load.

## Stakeholder agency

- Treat customers, buyers, users, partners, competitors, suppliers, staff, and funders as adaptive actors with alternatives and the ability to refuse, renegotiate, copy, delay, or leave.
- Do not translate stated interest, access, investor attention, or a founder narrative into commitment.
- Keep claims about competitor intent, customer motives, future cooperation, and market response as inference or forecast unless directly evidenced.

## Characteristic failure modes

- Solution-first reasoning: treating a built product as evidence of a valuable business.
- Demand theatre: promoting interviews, traffic, waitlists, or friendly pilots into willingness to pay or retention.
- Unit-economics laundering: using revenue, gross margin, or a blended average while omitting acquisition, service, concentration, churn, or working-capital costs.
- Scale before repeatability: adding spend, staff, markets, or features before the causal and operating mechanism survives.
- Total-addressable-market distraction: substituting a large category estimate for reachable buyers, credible distribution, and a viable wedge.
- Founder and sunk-cost bias: protecting identity, narrative, or past effort rather than comparing the next commitment with alternatives.
- Advantage fantasy: calling novelty, secrecy, features, or early access defensible without switching costs, network effects, learning, brand, regulation, cost, or distribution evidence.

## Out-of-scope boundaries

- Do not provide regulated financial, investment, tax, legal, accounting, medical, or clinical advice.
- Do not invent market size, competitor facts, customer behaviour, pricing, margins, or probabilities. Label supplied figures and forecasts by provenance.
- Do not turn personal purpose or household needs into a claim that the venture is commercially viable.
- Do not recommend deception, fabricated traction, undisclosed conflicts, coercive sales, unauthorised data use, or manipulation.

## Readiness implications

- **Ready:** the proposed bounded commitment has adequate problem, demand, economic, capability, and constraint evidence for its consequence, with downside controls and a credible advantage or learning objective.
- **Conditional:** proceeding is justified only through named stage limits, thresholds, permissions, economics, capacity, or stop conditions.
- **Not validated:** a load-bearing demand, payment, retention, cost, capability, distribution, or advantage claim lacks discriminating evidence. Test that claim before a larger commitment.
- **Infeasible as posed:** a demonstrated constraint, structurally negative mechanism, unavailable capability, or necessary stakeholder refusal blocks the exact model or horizon. Missing evidence alone is not infeasibility.

## Application checklist

1. State the exact commitment, customer, outcome, offer, horizon, and downside.
2. Map evidence across problem, payment, realised value, retention, economics, capability, distribution, and advantage.
3. Identify the weakest load-bearing claim, binding constraint, strongest rival, and best alternative use of capacity.
4. Choose the smallest discriminating commercial test or justified commitment.
5. Set success, failure, stop, cash/exposure, and review conditions without false precision.
