# Project / Product lens

Use this lens to decide whether a project or product should continue, be tested, pivot, stop, or change sequence. Apply the canonical reality protocol first; this file specialises the questions and evidence without redefining claim statuses or readiness states.

## Routing boundary

- Select this as the primary lens when the central decision concerns a project or product outcome, value proposition, scope, sequence, dependency, investment, experiment, pivot, or stop decision.
- Select it as a secondary lens only when a different supported professional decision is primary and project or product evidence materially changes the causal analysis, risk, or action.
- Route a commercial-model decision to the business/venture lens and an audience, channel, or growth decision to the marketing/growth lens. If either contains a separable project or product decision, use this lens only for that bounded part.
- Do not force a standalone personal, intimate, family, household, or general-life decision into a project metaphor. Use exact personal context when it changes a genuine project or product decision.
- Activate no additional lens merely to add perspective. Keep one primary lens and at most one materially relevant secondary lens.

## Supported decisions and outcomes

- Define the intended change in user, customer, operational, or organisational reality before discussing output, scope, activity, or delivery dates.
- Distinguish the target outcome from shipped features, completed tasks, meetings, adoption proxies, and effort.
- Decide among validated continuation, a bounded experiment, pivot, stop, or explicitly not-yet-validated action.
- Choose scope and sequence, identify the binding constraint, compare opportunity costs, and expose dependencies that can block the outcome.
- Set falsifiers, stop conditions, leading indicators, and a review horizon appropriate to the decision.

## Decision-relevant evidence

- Direct behaviour or outcome measures at the relevant population and time horizon, including baseline, trend, cohort, and counterfactual where available.
- Evidence for the user problem, willingness or ability to adopt, realised value, repeated use, substitution, and abandonment—not merely expressed enthusiasm.
- Delivery evidence: capacity, quality, cycle time, operational load, cost to complete, cost to maintain, and remaining work.
- Constraint and dependency evidence: decision rights, technical limits, legal or policy requirements, upstream owners, procurement, distribution, and critical dates.
- Failed, null, and contradictory results as well as successes. Record who produced each item, how it was measured, and what it can actually establish.
- Remaining cost and foregone alternatives. Treat past spend as history, not as a reason to spend the next unit.

## Causal mechanisms

- Trace the minimum plausible chain from intervention to outcome, such as awareness, access, first value, repeated value, retention, and aggregate impact.
- Identify necessary links, bottlenecks, feedback loops, delays, substitution effects, and ways local improvement can damage the wider system.
- Separate evidence that the problem exists from evidence that this intervention solves it and from evidence that the result can scale.
- Use bounded experiments to discriminate between live causal explanations. Prefer a test of the weakest load-bearing assumption over more delivery against an untested chain.
- Model dependencies as causal requirements with owners, incentives, failure modes, and alternatives—not as dates on a plan.

## Stakeholder agency

- Treat users, customers, sponsors, operators, delivery teams, and dependency owners as autonomous actors who may adopt, adapt, resist, renegotiate, substitute, or leave.
- Distinguish a stakeholder's recorded commitment from an inferred motive or forecast of cooperation.
- Expose any plan that requires ideal users, unquestioning sponsors, perfect delivery, or cooperative dependency owners. Validate that dependency, add a contingency, or lower readiness.
- Do not turn stakeholder labels into causal explanations. Ask what observable behaviour would distinguish preference, constraint, incentive, capability, and misunderstanding.

## Characteristic failure modes

- Output theatre: equating shipped scope, utilisation, velocity, or effort with the intended outcome.
- Demand fantasy: treating interviews, senior enthusiasm, sign-ups, or a convenient pilot as proof of durable value.
- Proxy capture: optimising the visible metric while the underlying outcome stalls or worsens.
- Sunk-cost protection and cherished-work bias: defending prior investment, identity, or authorship instead of comparing future value and cost.
- Dependency optimism: assuming another team, platform, policy owner, or channel will cooperate on schedule without current evidence or fallback.
- Scale-before-mechanism: extrapolating from a selected cohort, novelty effect, or unsupported causal story.
- False precision: presenting a delivery forecast, adoption forecast, or benefit estimate more precisely than the evidence supports.

## Out-of-scope boundaries

- Do not use this lens as a substitute for the business/venture or marketing/growth lens, or for legal, clinical, financial, or safety expertise.
- Do not infer that commercial viability follows from user value, or that user value follows from delivery success.
- Do not pretend every whole-person decision is a project. Apply the core protocol directly when no project/product lens is needed.
- Do not claim an unsupported domain is covered because its situation can be described as a project.

## Readiness implications

- **Ready:** the target outcome is explicit; current evidence supports the load-bearing value and causal assumptions; binding constraints and dependencies are understood; and the proposed action has proportionate downside controls.
- **Conditional:** material support exists, but named contingencies, thresholds, permissions, dependencies, or monitoring must hold. State them as conditions, not optimism.
- **Not validated:** a load-bearing problem, value, adoption, causal, scale, cost, or dependency assumption lacks discriminating evidence. Recommend the cheapest decisive test, a bounded hold, or a reversible next move.
- **Infeasible as posed:** current evidence establishes a conflict with a binding constraint or a necessary causal link. Name the exact conflict and test a materially different path; missing evidence alone is not infeasibility.
- A project can be ready to stop or pivot even when it is not ready to scale. Classify the decision actually under consideration.

## Application checklist

1. Restate the outcome and horizon independently of activity and output.
2. Identify the weakest load-bearing value or causal assumption and the binding constraint.
3. Compare current evidence, contradictions, dependencies, remaining cost, and best alternative.
4. Challenge sunk-cost or cherished-work reasoning explicitly when present.
5. Choose continuation, bounded experiment, pivot, stop, or not-yet-validated action and state its falsifier and review point.
